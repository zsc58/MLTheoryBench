# Reference Proof

## Source Contract and Repair Boundary

The relevant source closure consists of the population setting and standing causal assumptions, the attribute kernel and conditional-independence assumption, and the projection result. The source's observational i.i.d. data are used to learn an APO predictor; the Monte Carlo pairs in this task instead follow the specified randomized assignment law. No property of a particular training algorithm, no moment-balancing result, and no consistency assumption for an arbitrary fitted predictor is inherited.

The original source at L001137 uses the population symbol `g(t,t')` for a finite-sample joint-treatment estimator. Its surrounding estimation language and subsequent population calculation determine the local correction: distinguish the estimator from its mean and limit. Exact-match joint-treatment weighting is meaningful only at positive joint atoms. The conditional-law argument already present at L001133 and L001139-L001149 supplies the general-space proof. The unmatched delimiter at L001147 is a typographical error. These source repairs remain recorded under theory_009.

The source at L001131-L001134 specifies a randomized experiment whose joint outcome/covariate law at assigned treatment t uses the population law of `(Y(t),X)`. The public task realizes this experiment on the full-population product extension `P*(dω,dt,du)=P(dω)p_T(dt)K(t,du)`. It does not define the experiment merely by independence of T from the σ-field generated by the fixed-index potential outcomes. For an uncountable treatment index, that weaker independence need not control the random-index evaluation `Y(T)`, even when the evaluation map is measurable with respect to the full population σ-field times the treatment σ-field. The product extension supplies the actual assignment mechanism; no extra sample-path or separability assumption is needed.

This distinction resolves the diagonal example. If `Y(t,ω)=1{ω=t}` on the uniform population space `[0,2]`, each fixed potential outcome is zero almost surely. Using the observational coupling `T=ω` gives observed outcome one, but is not the specified randomized product experiment. Under the product experiment the population coordinate and the assigned treatment coordinate are independent, the diagonal has probability zero, and the randomized outcome and its projected mean are zero. Thus the population example is not excluded; its intervention is evaluated under the correct law.

Conditional laws are versions, so the projection conclusion is almost everywhere in the attribute law, not pointwise for arbitrary versions at null attribute values. The public domain D states the necessary finite conditional outcome mean explicitly. In particular, the proof must not silently assume the stronger condition `E*[|Y|]<∞` across all attribute values.

## Theorem-Critical Obligations

1. Use the specified full-population product experiment and establish its randomized integration formula.
2. Derive that its conditional outcome distribution, and hence its conditional mean, given both treatment variables depends only on T.
3. Obtain the projection identity on the explicitly defined finite-mean domain, with the correct almost-everywhere qualification and a finite right-hand side.
4. Verify integrability and positive attribute mass for the Monte Carlo ratio, then establish its eventual definition and convergence in the oracle case.
5. Identify the fitted-value ratio as a plug-in statistic without asserting unsupported consistency for arbitrary fitted predictors.

All five obligations are closed below.

## Audited Standard Background

The following are standard_independent background facts, not specialized or paper-specific dependencies.

- A probability measure and a probability kernel define an iterated probability measure. Integration against that measure agrees with iterated integration for nonnegative measurable functions. Multiplication by a further probability measure defines the corresponding independent product experiment.
- Tonelli's theorem permits exchanging nonnegative measurable integrals under probability measures and probability kernels, allowing the value infinity. Fubini's theorem permits the corresponding signed exchange when the absolute integral is finite.
- A measurable probability kernel `H_z` is a conditional law of a real random variable Y given Z if, for every measurable set C in the state space of Z and every Borel outcome set A, `P(Z∈C,Y∈A)=∫_C H_z(A) P_Z(dz)`.
- A regular conditional probability kernel integrates nonnegative measurable functions to versions of their conditional expectations. Such conditional expectations are unique almost surely. Signed kernel means are obtained by subtraction when their conditional absolute means are finite. Iterated conditioning applies to nonnegative variables and to integrable signed variables.
- The strong law of large numbers states that for i.i.d. real variables `Z_i` with `E[|Z_1|]<∞`, their sample average converges almost surely to `E[Z_1]`.

The measurability, finiteness, and sampling hypotheses needed for these facts are verified at their applications below.

## Complete Proof

### 1. Randomized law and conditional-independence collapse

The randomized sample space is explicitly the product of the original population space and the treatment/attribute assignment space. In particular, the population σ-field used here is the full `𝓕`, not merely the σ-field generated by fixed-index outcomes. The map `Y(ω,t,u)=Y(t,ω)` is measurable on this product space by the stated joint measurability assumption.

For every nonnegative measurable function `φ(t,u,x,y)`, the definition of `P*` therefore gives

`E*[φ(T,T',X,Y)] = ∫_𝒯 p_T(dt) ∫_𝒜 K(t,du) E_P[φ(t,u,X,Y(t))]`.

Indeed, substitute the coordinate definitions of T, T', X, and Y into the integral against `P(dω)p_T(dt)K(t,du)`, and apply nonnegative iterated integration. This establishes the formula from the defined sampling experiment, rather than inferring it from coordinate-family independence.

For each t, let `H_t` be the population distribution of `Y(t)`. It is a probability kernel: for each Borel set A, `H_t(A)=∫_Ω 1{Y(t,ω)∈A}P(dω)` is measurable in t by joint measurability and nonnegative integration. For any measurable set C of treatment/attribute pairs,

`P*((T,T')∈C,Y∈A)=∫_𝒯 p_T(dt) ∫_𝒜 K(t,du) 1_C(t,u) H_t(A)`.

Thus `(t,u)↦H_t` is a conditional outcome law given `(T,T')`. Its mean is

`∫ y H_t(dy)=E_P[Y(t)]=g(t)`.

It is independent of u. The mean is finite because `E_P[|Y(t)|]<∞`. This is the required collapse of the joint-treatment mean, derived from the population-independent assignment mechanism and the fact that the attribute kernel uses only T. It is a statement about the randomized law, not the generally confounded observational conditional mean. No claim is needed at incompatible treatment-attribute pairs, where conditional versions are unrestricted.

For completeness, the corrected source estimator at a positive joint atom `q=P*(T=t,T'=u)>0` is

`m̂_n(t,u)=n^(-1) Σ_i Y_i 1{T_i=t,T'_i=u}/q`.

The collapse gives `E*[Y 1{T=t,T'=u}/q]=g(t)` and `E*[|Y| 1{T=t,T'=u}/q]=E_P[|Y(t)|]<∞`. Thus `E*[m̂_n(t,u)]=g(t)` and the strong law gives `m̂_n(t,u)→g(t)` almost surely for i.i.d. randomized observations. Finite-sample equality with g is not asserted. This estimator is not used for continuous-treatment proof steps.

### 2. Projection with local, rather than global, integrability

Define the nonnegative measurable functions

`b_+(t)=E_P[max(Y(t),0)]`,

`b_-(t)=E_P[max(-Y(t),0)]`, and

`a(t)=E_P[|Y(t)|]=b_+(t)+b_-(t)`.

All are finite for every t, and `g(t)=b_+(t)-b_-(t)` is measurable with `|g(t)|≤a(t)`.

For any nonnegative measurable outcome function h, put `b_h(t)=E_P[h(Y(t))]`. For every measurable attribute set B, the randomized construction and the definitions of the two regular conditional laws give

`∫_B [∫ h(y) R_u(dy)] p_{T'}(du)`

`= E*[h(Y) 1{T'∈B}]`

`= ∫_𝒯 b_h(t) K(t,B) p_T(dt)`

`= ∫_B [∫ b_h(t) Q_u(dt)] p_{T'}(du)`.

All these equalities concern nonnegative integrands, so they remain valid without a global first moment. Equivalently, both bracketed functions are versions of the same nonnegative conditional expectation. Almost-sure uniqueness therefore identifies them for `p_{T'}`-almost every u.

Apply this argument to the three functions `h(y)=|y|`, `h(y)=max(y,0)`, and `h(y)=max(-y,0)`. Outside the union of their three null sets,

`∫ |y| R_u(dy)=∫ a(t) Q_u(dt)`,

and the corresponding positive- and negative-part identities also hold.

If such a u belongs to D, the first display is finite. Hence `∫ |g(t)| Q_u(dt)≤∫ a(t) Q_u(dt)<∞`, and subtraction of the positive- and negative-part identities is legitimate. It yields

`g'(u)=∫ y R_u(dy)=∫ [b_+(t)-b_-(t)] Q_u(dt)=∫ g(t) Q_u(dt)`.

This is exactly the requested projection identity for `p_{T'}`-almost every u in D. The proof is the conditional-independence collapse followed by iterated averaging, with nonnegative parts used only to handle the stated local integrability domain. It also proves that the right-hand side is finite wherever the asserted identity applies.

A positive-mass attribute atom cannot lie in an exceptional set of zero attribute probability. Thus the identity holds at every fixed atom in D with positive mass. Deterministic attributes are covered because a point-mass kernel is a probability kernel; no density of T' or additional argument is required. Values on attribute-null sets remain version-dependent.

### 3. Oracle Monte Carlo convergence and zero counts

Fix the discrete attribute value u in the task and write `q=p_{T'}({u})>0`. Define

`V_i=1{T'_i=u}`, `U_i=V_i g(T_i)`, and `N_n=Σ_i V_i`.

The pairs `(T_i,T'_i)` are i.i.d. under the randomized assignment law, so the pairs `(U_i,V_i)` are i.i.d. By the positive-atom conclusion and conditional integrability proved above,

`E[|U_i|]=q ∫ |g(t)| Q_u(dt)<∞`,

`E[U_i]=q ∫ g(t) Q_u(dt)=q g'(u)`, and

`E[V_i]=q`.

Also `0≤V_i≤1`, so both scalar strong-law hypotheses hold. Therefore, almost surely,

`n^(-1) Σ_i U_i → q g'(u)` and `N_n/n → q`.

Since q is strictly positive, eventually `N_n/n>q/2`; in particular the ratio is eventually defined almost surely. Dividing the two convergent sequences gives

`[Σ_i 1{T'_i=u} g(T_i)]/[Σ_i 1{T'_i=u}] → g'(u)` almost surely.

This also gives convergence in probability. At finite n the zero-count event has probability `(1-q)^n`; it is not legitimate to assume every finite sample has a positive denominator. If a value is assigned to the statistic on this event, any fixed such extension leaves the stated limit unchanged. The case q=1 is included and has no zero-count samples for n≥1. The case q=0 is excluded by the task.

### 4. Plug-in interpretation

For a fitted measurable function ĝ, replace the unavailable values g(T_i) in the same empirical conditional average by ĝ(T_i). This gives precisely the displayed plug-in estimator on its positive-denominator event. This is a definition of a plug-in estimate, not a consistency theorem for an arbitrary predictor. The proof does not condition on a random fitted predictor and then assume independence of the Monte Carlo draws without justification. No training-sample independence or predictor accuracy is needed for the oracle case ĝ=g proved above, and no learned-predictor convergence claim is made.
